3D-TrIm — single-virus tracking with co-registered cellular imaging.

A microscope that follows a single virus through 3D space at 10³ localizations per second while a second imaging arm captures the surrounding cellular environment — resolving the earliest moments of virus–cell interaction in live cells and tissue.

Most microscopes face a hard trade-off: track fast, or image broad. A virus diffusing at the cell surface moves microns in milliseconds: far too quickly for a volumetric scan to capture. Yet, understanding what it's doing requires seeing the cellular landscape around it. 3D-TrIm resolves both at once.

The problem

Single-particle tracking can localize a fluorescent particle with nanometer precision at kilohertz rates, but only in a tiny focal volume which renders the cellular context invisible. Volumetric microscopy can image a whole cell, but at frame rates far too slow to keep up with a freely diffusing virus. The result: we could watch a virus or its environment, never both, and never at the critical moment of first contact.

How 3D-TrIm works

  1. Lock onto a single particle

    A real-time feedback loop drives a piezo stage to keep a single fluorescent virus centered in the focal spot, reading out its position thousands of times per second.

  2. Image the environment in parallel

    A second, independent imaging arm continuously captures the surrounding 3D cellular volume that the virus is exploring.

  3. Co-register trajectory to volume

    The particle's high-speed trajectory and the slower cellular volume are stitched into a single co-registered dataset, placing every step of the virus in its true structural context.

For the first time we could watch a virus "skim" along the cell surface — sampling actin-rich protrusions before committing — in its full three-dimensional context.

What we found

The co-registered data revealed the first direct observation of virus "skimming" behavior: viruses sampling actin-rich cellular protrusions through distinct diffusional modes that depend on the local surface architecture. Extending the system to live, multilayer epithelial tissue showed these dynamics persist in more physiologically realistic settings... and in our most recent work resolved actin-aided trafficking of SARS-CoV-2 on the plasma membrane.

Why it matters

The start point of infection: how a virus searches, contacts, and commits to a cell,  has been largely invisible to live imaging. By making that moment observable in 3D, 3D-TrIm turns a previously inaccessible window of virus–cell interaction into quantitative data, with implications for how we understand viral entry and design interventions against it.
