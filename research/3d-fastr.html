3D-FASTR — tessellating point-scan imaging.

Point-scanning microscopes remain the workhorses of biology, yet scanning 3D volumes point-by-point is agonizingly slow. 3D-FASTR is an unconventional approach to point-volume scanning: using tessellating patterns instead of plane-by-plane, optimized-sparse point patterns capture 3D volumes 4–8× faster than conventional z-stacks and pair naturally with active-feedback tracking systems.

Point-scan microscopes — confocal, two-photon — image deep and clean, but rarely fast. They visit the sample one point at a time along a rigid raster, and building a 3D volume means grinding through plane after plane. 3D-FASTR rethinks the scan pattern itself.

The problem

To capture a volume, a conventional point-scan microscope sweeps a full 2D raster, steps the focus, and repeats — spending most of its time re-measuring regions that haven't changed. For anything dynamic, the volume is stale before it finishes. The scan pattern, not the detector, is the bottleneck.

How 3D-FASTR works

  1. Tessellate the volume

    Instead of a plane-by-plane raster, distribute sample points across all three dimensions in a tessellating pattern that tiles space efficiently and leaves no large gaps.

  2. Sample sparsely, but optimally

    An optimized linear sparse-sampling pattern places far fewer points than a full raster while preserving the information needed to reconstruct the volume.

  3. Reconstruct the full volume

    Because the pattern fills space evenly, the sparse measurements interpolate into a continuous 3D volume — at a fraction of the acquisition time.

Break the scan out of the raster, and a point-scan microscope can fill a volume in the time it used to spend on a few planes.

What we built

A generalizable theory for multi-dimensional tessellating scan patterns, and a volumetric two-photon laser-scanning microscope demonstrating a 4–8× speedup over conventional z-stack acquisition. Because it accelerates the scan rather than the hardware, 3D-FASTR slots directly into active-feedback systems — the natural partner to single-particle tracking microscopes like 3D-TrIm.
