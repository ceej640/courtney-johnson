Phase-diversity wavefront sensing for fluorescence microscopy.

A rapid, image-based adaptive-optics approach that recovers the optical wavefront directly from the images a microscope already takes — no Shack–Hartmann sensor, no guide star — broadly compatible across modalities and extended to 3D and dual-view light sheet.

Image quality deep inside living samples is limited by optical aberrations that vary in space and time. Correcting them — adaptive optics — usually means dedicated wavefront-sensing hardware and a bright reference point. Phase diversity removes both requirements, recovering the wavefront from the sample itself.

The problem

As light travels through tissue it accumulates aberrations that blur the image and scramble fine structure. Classical adaptive optics borrows from astronomy: a Shack–Hartmann sensor measures the wavefront from a guide star. But biological samples rarely offer a convenient point source, and adding a hardware sensor complicates every microscope it touches.

How phase diversity works

  1. Acquire phase-diverse images

    Capture two or more images of the same scene with a known, deliberate difference between them — typically a calibrated defocus applied by a deformable mirror.

  2. Jointly estimate object and wavefront

    A Gauss–Newton optimizer solves for the unknown specimen and the unknown aberration simultaneously — the known diversity between frames makes the problem well-posed.

  3. Correct — in hardware or in software

    The recovered wavefront drives a deformable mirror to physically cancel the aberration, or, with DeepPD, feeds a learned model that restores the image computationally.

The microscope becomes its own wavefront sensor — aberrations are read out of the very images we already collect.

What we built

Prototype widefield and asymmetric dual-view light-sheet microscopes implementing a Gauss–Newton phase-diverse estimator, validated on cellular structures. We then extended the method to 3D samples and built DeepPD — a neural model of the deformable mirror's response that enables joint phase-and-object estimation and opens a path to computational, hardware-free aberration correction.

Why it matters

Because phase diversity needs no special hardware and no guide star, it can be added to microscopes that could never host a traditional wavefront sensor — broadening access to high-resolution imaging at depth. The computational direction, DeepPD, points toward microscopes that correct themselves with software alone.
